DuskRead

The app, and the design system behind it

A reading habit that survives the news cycle

It opens in Ink: a dark page with the hue drained out of it entirely, where lightness, weight and spacing are the only things left to separate one element from another. The alternative is Paper Black — the same layout with a single terracotta accent lit, and that accent means exactly one thing: there is sound here. Nothing else in the interface is ever allowed to be coloured.

What it holds: you find something good at eleven in the morning and you are not going to read it at eleven in the morning. DuskRead takes the link, keeps the blogs you follow next to it, and has the pile sorted out by the evening — so there is one screen to open when you finally sit down.

This page is both halves of that — what the app does, and the visual language it is built from. The switch in the top corner repaints every screen below between the two schemes; it is worth flipping once.

What it does

Four things, in the order they matter

Reading is the point. Everything else on this list exists to get a piece of writing in front of you, or to keep you there once it is.

Save a link

Paste it or share it from anywhere. It is usable the instant it lands and fills in its own real title afterwards, so nothing waits on a network round-trip you did not ask for.

Follow a blog

Give it a site and it finds the RSS or Atom feed itself. New posts arrive as a digest line per blog — a count, not a carousel — and expand in place when you want to look.

Ask what is in it

A summary written on the phone by Gemini Nano, from the reader or from a swipe on a list row. The article never leaves the device, and the panel floats rather than taking you anywhere.

Sit with it

A timer at the bottom of the screen, drawn as a meter rather than written as a word. Phone face-down, twenty-five minutes, actually read the thing.

And readback, when you would rather listen. Posts handed to the separate readback project come back as audio on the device, and DuskRead plays them — read-only, never writing to that library. It is the one feature with a dependency outside the app, so it sits after the four above rather than in front of them, and its screen is documented further down.

Screen — Home

What is there to read, without going to look

The screen the app opens on, and the one that answers “what is there to read” without making you go and look. Four sections, in this order: something from saved, whatever is mid-readback, the focus presets, and the following digest — everything the app knows, on one screen, before you have chosen a tab.

Sections sit flush on the background, separated by their own bottom hairline. No card boxes anywhere, and no nested carousel of tiles. Two of the eyebrows carry a leading glyph rather than text alone: the waveform on Today’s readback, and on Focus the play or pause of the timer’s own state, so a running session is legible from the section label before you read a word of it.

Screen — Saved

Unread and read, told apart without a heading

The pile itself. A link arrives by paste or by the system share sheet and is in the list immediately, under whatever title the URL gives away, backfilling the real one when the fetch lands — the list is never blocked on the network.

A read link recedes rather than being struck through: one alpha over the whole row, plus a trailing tick. Unread rows carry no trailing icon at all.

The address bar takes a site, not a feed URL. Feed discovery runs against whatever you paste, because nobody has the /atom.xml to hand when they decide to follow something.

A digest, not a carousel. The preview was a horizontal strip of boxed cards once — the last boxed surface left on Home, and boxed precisely because a strip has to agree on a card width, so it could show two and clip the third. Flat rows have no such constraint.

Three, then a door. The preview stops at three and hands over to the full topic screen with an accented ALL n POSTS row, so a section inside a dashboard cannot push everything under it off the bottom of the screen.

Screen — Article summary

Enough to decide whether to read it

The question this answers is not "what does this article say" but "is this worth the next twenty minutes" — and that one framing decides the rest of the screen. It runs on the phone: Gemini Nano through AICore, reading text the app has already extracted for the reader view, so nothing is uploaded and nothing needs a connection.

Netflix currently runs two different autoscalers for Apache Flink, and they are actively moving toward replacing the custom one with the open-source Kubernetes-native autoscaler.

The custom autoscaler predates the Kubernetes operator by several years. It was built when the platform ran Flink on its own scheduling layer, and it carries assumptions from that era — most visibly that scaling decisions are made from a single aggregated backlog metric rather than per-vertex.

Feature parity is the gating question. The open-source autoscaler reached rough equivalence on throughput-based scaling last year, but two things kept the custom one alive: its handling of sources with irregular partition counts, and the operational tooling built around it.

Prose, not bullets. A summary of an argument delivered as fragments asks you to reassemble the argument, which is most of the work you opened the panel to avoid. One paragraph, one tone.

Two lengths, both real. Short and Full are genuinely different answers from the model rather than one trimmed to fit the other, and they are named by what you get to read.

In the toolbar, and hidden until it can work. A summary needs the article's text, which exists only once the reader has extracted it — so the button lives where that has already happened, and is hidden rather than greyed out before then. A list row can ask too: Saved already teaches swipe-left-to-remove, so swipe-right-to-summarise mirrors a gesture that exists, and triaging a list is the moment a summary is worth most.

Floating, and never a destination. The panel sits over the article, the icon becomes a spinner in its own slot while the model runs, and nothing else on the screen moves. Checking a summary never costs your place in the thing you were reading. On platforms with no on-device model the control is simply not drawn.

Screen — Focus

A meter, not a word in a void

The part of the app you look at least. Pick a length, put the phone down, read the thing you saved — so the screen's job is to be glanceable from a metre away on a desk, and to be startable without looking at all.

Which is why it is bottom-anchored in the thumb zone rather than centred, and why the countdown is drawn with the same bars as every waveform in the app: elapsed filled, remaining not. One visual language covers audio and time alike, so a bar that is two-thirds lit means the same thing on both screens.

Screen — Readback

Where the accent earns its keep

The one screen that depends on something outside the app: audio a sync step has already put on the device, from the separate readback project, read-only. It is the smaller half of what DuskRead is for — but it is where the single accent hue is defined, so the rest of the system is easier to read once this screen is.

Two facts per row, not five — the third fact, how long this actually is, is drawn as a waveform rather than written out. The playing row is the only coloured thing on screen: title, position and its waveform's filled fraction all switch to the accent, everything else stays grey.

Wide — desktop and web

One breakpoint, and the bar stops floating

Every design decision on this page assumes a thumb and a phone held in one hand. A mouse and a 1180dp window break all three assumptions at once: nothing is reached by reaching, a line of text will run to a hundred characters if you let it, and a bar floating at the bottom of a window nobody is holding is just a bar in the wrong place. This section is what the same system looks like when those assumptions go.

Android is the app; the other three are the workshop. The breakpoint, the rail, the bottom transport and the capped measure ship today; the two-pane split below is drawn and not yet built. Read this section as an argument about the system rather than a screen anyone is using daily.

BreakpointLayout.TwoPaneBreakpoint = 720.dp — one threshold, not a five-class ladder. Below it the phone layout is used unchanged, including in a narrow desktop window; above it, two panes.
RailLayout.RailWidth = 64.dp, left, hairline on its right edge. The three tabs go vertical, the theme toggle and Settings sit at the far end — a column of empty rail separates them, so no divider is needed the way the floating bar needs one.
List paneLayout.ListPaneWidth = 400.dp. Fixed, not proportional — a list of two-line titles has a correct width and a 2560px monitor does not change it.
Detail paneFills the rest; its content stops at Layout.ReadingMeasure = 640.dp, left-aligned against the pane's own gutter rather than centred in the window.
GuttersReadingGutter 18 → WideReadingGutter 28dp, ListGutter 14 → WideListGutter 20dp. Type sizes do not change at all.
TransportFull-bleed along the bottom, above nothing. The only element that keeps its phone position, because it outlives whichever pane is open.

The bar stops floating. On a phone it sits at the bottom because that is where the thumb is. On a desktop there is no thumb, the pointer is already wherever the eye is, and the bottom edge of an 820dp window is simply the longest journey from the list — so navigation goes to a left rail and only the transport stays bottom, full-bleed, because it is the one thing that outlives the pane above it.

The list pane is a fixed width, not a fraction. Two-line titles have a correct measure and a wider monitor does not change it; a percentage would just make the same four rows emptier.

Nothing gets bigger. Titles stay at their phone size, the waveform keeps its 2dp bars, rows keep their hairline. Only the gutters open up. Scaling type with the window is the fastest way to turn a considered phone screen into a generic desktop one.

Monochrome

Ink — a second design, not the first one with the colour deleted

The hue drops to nothing and level is carried by luminance alone — the interface was already greyscale, so the idea survives intact. In the app it is reachable from the tab bar's contrast toggle or from Settings, and the choice persists across restarts. It is also the default, which is why this page opens in it.

The frame below is pinned to Ink. Every other mockup on the page follows the Paper Black / Ink switch in the top corner — flip it and scroll back up to see the same screens with the accent lit.

Everything that carries the accent in Paper Black — the selected pill, the playing row's title and position, its chip border — becomes white here. Nothing changes shape, weight or position; only hue disappears, which is the test of whether the layout was ever relying on colour to do its work.

Brand

Dusk bar — one shape, a crescent bitten out

The house language ("the waveform is the alphabet") carries into the mark itself: one wide bar — read, listen — with a crescent bitten from its top, for dusk. One mark, in Ink, at every size: the terracotta launcher pair it used to swap with is gone.

The crescent is painted as an opaque circle in the ground colour, drawn over the bar rather than subtracted from it — a bite that extends past the shape it is taken out of cannot be a hole. The mark settles into place on boot, the outer bars arriving last.

Icon set

Bar — the waveform is the alphabet

All twenty-two, drawn from ui/theme/DuskReadIcons.kt's own path data rather than redrawn to match it. One stroke spec throughout: 2.4 weight, round cap and join, nothing filled. Every glyph that can be built from evenly spaced vertical bars is — Waveform, Feed, Offline, Settings (the sliders are bars), the shading on Contrast — because the waveform is the one visual idea this app has, and a set drawn from it agrees with the data on screen rather than merely sitting beside it. What cannot be built that way — Target, Shuffle, the folder shapes — borrows the same weight and round terminal so the set still looks cut from one clip.

BookmarkFilled is the single filled glyph, and only ever the on half of a pair with Bookmark. A control with two states has to be told apart at icon size, and a tint change is a colour difference — the one thing Ink deliberately does not have. Filled and hollow survive the scheme swap; terracotta and grey do not.

Type

Jost and Inconsolata, five roles

Jost for everything the eye reads as language — a 1920s geometric with real character where a system sans would have none. Inconsolata for every number and every meta line, so data never has to borrow the platform's default mono. Four Jost weights are bundled (400 through 700) and no light cut: at the sizes this app uses, a light weight on a near-black ground disappears.

The section label is the one that gets confused. It is uppercase and tightly tracked, which reads as monospace at a glance — but it is Jost, and the mono next to it is doing a different job. Inconsolata marks data: a duration, a word count, a host, a number of new posts. If a label is naming a section rather than reporting a value, it is not mono.

Get it

One clone, one Gradle task

Android is the app — it is what this is built and tested against, day to day, on a phone. Saved links, feeds, the timer and both schemes work the moment it opens; nothing needs configuring first.

git clone https://github.com/MKS-01/duskread.git && cd duskread
./gradlew :androidApp:installDebug  # ~5s once warm
Android8.0 Oreo and up. The target that is finished.
SummariesNeed a phone with AICore — Pixel 9 and up, Galaxy S24 and up, and similar. Everywhere else the control simply is not drawn.
ReadbackAudio comes from the separate readback project, synced onto the device. Without it the tab is empty rather than hidden.
Desktop, web, iOSBuild from the same source and exist to prove it travels. The workshop, not the product.

A personal project, built one-handed on a phone on a walk, and kept that way on purpose. The source is MIT.
